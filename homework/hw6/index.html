Statistical Independence

Independence in probability

Two events $A$ and $B$ are independent when the probability that both occur is the product of their probabilities:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$$

If $P(B) > 0$ this is the same as $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$: learning that $B$ occurred does not change the probability of $A$. The definition is symmetric, and if $A$ and $B$ are independent so are $A$ and $B^c$, $A^c$ and $B$, $A^c$ and $B^c$.

Two discrete random variables $X$ and $F$ are independent when every pair of events $\{X = x\}$, $\{F = f\}$ is independent, that is, when the joint distribution factorizes into the product of the marginals:

$$P(X = x_i,\ F = f_j) = P(X = x_i)\,P(F = f_j) \qquad \text{for every } i, j$$

Equivalently, the conditional distribution of $F$ given $X = x_i$ is the same for every $i$, and equal to the marginal distribution of $F$:

$$P(F = f_j \mid X = x_i) = P(F = f_j) \qquad \text{for every } i, j$$

A few consequences are worth keeping in mind.

  • Independence is a property of the whole joint distribution. One cell satisfying the product rule is not enough: it must hold in all $r \times c$ cells.
  • For numerical variables, independence implies $\operatorname{Cov}(X, F) = 0$, but not conversely: $X$ uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $F = X^2$ are uncorrelated and completely dependent. For categorical variables there is no covariance at all, and the factorization is the only meaningful notion.
  • Pairwise independence of three variables does not imply mutual independence, and independence of $X$ and $F$ does not imply conditional independence given a third variable $Z$, nor the other way round. The last point is the heart of the causality section below.

Independence in statistics

In a sample of $n$ observations of the pair $(X, F)$ the probabilities are replaced by frequencies. Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of observations with $X = x_i$ and $F = f_j$, and $n_{i\cdot} = \sum_j n_{ij}$, $n_{\cdot j} = \sum_i n_{ij}$ the totals of row $i$ and column $j$. The contingency table collects the $n_{ij}$ with these margins, and from it we read three kinds of relative frequency:

FrequencyFormulaEstimates
Joint$f_{ij} = n_{ij}/n$$P(X = x_i, F = f_j)$
Marginal$f_{i\cdot} = n_{i\cdot}/n,\quad f_{\cdot j} = n_{\cdot j}/n$$P(X = x_i),\ P(F = f_j)$
Conditional (row profile)$f_{j \mid i} = n_{ij}/n_{i\cdot}$$P(F = f_j \mid X = x_i)$
Conditional (column profile)$f_{i \mid j} = n_{ij}/n_{\cdot j}$$P(X = x_i \mid F = f_j)$

The two variables are statistically independent in the sample (perfect independence) when the joint frequencies are the product of the marginal ones, $f_{ij} = f_{i\cdot}\,f_{\cdot j}$, or, multiplying by $n$, when every count equals

$$\boxed{e_{ij} = \frac{n_{i\cdot}\, n_{\cdot j}}{n}}$$

which is again the same as saying that all the row profiles coincide with the marginal distribution of $F$. These $e_{ij}$ are the expected frequencies under independence: the table we would see if the margins were the observed ones and $X$ carried no information about $F$. They keep the margins, $\sum_j e_{ij} = n_{i\cdot}$ and $\sum_i e_{ij} = n_{\cdot j}$.

Even when $X$ and $F$ are truly independent, a random sample almost never satisfies $n_{ij} = e_{ij}$ exactly: the $e_{ij}$ are usually not even integers. The question is therefore whether the observed deviations are larger than sampling variability would produce. This is a hypothesis test of

$$H_0:\ p_{ij} = p_{i\cdot}\, p_{\cdot j}\ \text{for all } i, j \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_1:\ p_{ij} \ne p_{i\cdot}\, p_{\cdot j}\ \text{for some } i, j$$

Pearson's chi-square statistic

Under $H_0$ the maximum-likelihood estimates of $p_{i\cdot}$ and $p_{\cdot j}$ are the marginal frequencies, so $e_{ij}$ is the estimated expected count of cell $(i, j)$. Pearson's statistic adds the squared deviations, each scaled by its expected count:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(n_{ij} - e_{ij})^2}{e_{ij}} = n\left(\sum_{i,j} \frac{n_{ij}^2}{n_{i\cdot}\, n_{\cdot j}} - 1\right)$$

It is zero exactly under perfect independence and grows with every departure from it. The scaling by $e_{ij}$ is natural: a count is roughly Poisson, with variance close to its mean, so each term is the square of an approximately standardized deviation. When $n$ is large and $H_0$ holds, $\chi^2$ follows approximately a chi-square distribution with

$$\nu = (r - 1)(c - 1)$$

degrees of freedom: the $rc - 1$ free cell probabilities, minus the $(r - 1) + (c - 1)$ marginal probabilities estimated from the data. Equivalently, once the margins are fixed, only $(r-1)(c-1)$ counts can be chosen freely; the last row and column are determined by the totals.

The p-value is the probability, under $H_0$, of a statistic at least as large as the observed one, $p = P(\chi^2_\nu \ge \chi^2_{\text{obs}})$. At significance level $\alpha$ we reject independence when $p < \alpha$, or equivalently when $\chi^2_{\text{obs}}$ exceeds the critical value $\chi^2_{\nu,\,1-\alpha}$.

Conditions and complements

  • Independent observations. Each record must be a separate unit. Fifty failed logins from the same bot are not fifty independent observations, and they inflate $\chi^2$.
  • Large expected counts. The chi-square approximation is reliable when all $e_{ij} \ge 5$ (Cochran's rule allows up to 20% of cells between 1 and 5). Otherwise one merges categories, or uses an exact or permutation test.
  • Strength is not significance. $\chi^2$ grows linearly with $n$ for fixed proportions, so it measures the evidence, not the size of the association. Cramér's V rescales it to $[0, 1]$: $$V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n\,(\min(r, c) - 1)}}$$ with $V = 0$ under perfect independence and $V = 1$ when one variable determines the other.
  • Where the dependence lies. The adjusted residuals $\dfrac{n_{ij} - e_{ij}}{\sqrt{e_{ij}\,(1 - f_{i\cdot})(1 - f_{\cdot j})}}$ are approximately standard normal under $H_0$, so cells with $|r_{ij}| > 2$ are those that deviate most from independence.

The chi-square statistic and the p-value

Contributions $(n_{ij} - e_{ij})^2 / e_{ij}$

The p-value is computed from the regularized incomplete gamma function, $P(\chi^2_\nu \ge x) = Q(\nu/2,\ x/2)$, with a series for small $x$ and a continued fraction in the tail, so that very small p-values keep their significant digits instead of rounding to zero. It matches the textbook values: $P(\chi^2_1 \ge 3.841) = 0.0500$ and $\chi^2_{3,\,0.95} = 7.815$.

A check without the approximation: permutation test

Shuffling the $F$ labels among the records keeps both margins and destroys any link between $X$ and $F$. Each shuffle therefore produces a table drawn from the null hypothesis. The fraction of shuffled tables whose $\chi^2$ is at least the observed one is a p-value that does not rely on the chi-square approximation; it is useful when some expected counts are small.

Interpretation

All datasets at once

The four generated datasets behave as their models say. The authentication log and the network flows are dependent beyond any reasonable doubt, with p-values of order $10^{-27}$ and $10^{-50}$. Their Cramér's V is only around 0.2–0.25, "weak" on Cohen's scale, which is a reminder that V summarizes the whole table: a failure rate of 35% at night against 10% during the day is operationally a very relevant difference. The control, where independence is true by construction, gives a p-value around 0.4: no reason to reject $H_0$, as it should be. The phishing campaign, with only 400 employees, sits in between: the dependence is detected at the 1% level but not at the 0.1% level, and it rests on a few cells, mainly the IT department, which reports the e-mail more often and clicks less.

"Not rejected" does not mean "independent"

A large p-value says that the data are compatible with independence, not that independence has been proved: a weak dependence in a small sample goes undetected. Conversely, with $\alpha = 0.05$ about one test in twenty on truly independent variables rejects $H_0$ by chance, which matters when many pairs of log fields are screened at once.

Significance, strength and sample size

Multiplying every count by $k$ keeps all the conditional and marginal frequencies, and therefore the strength of the association, exactly as they are. But $\chi^2$ is multiplied by $k$ too, while the critical value does not change:

The same proportions can be "not significant" with a small sample and "extremely significant" with a large one, while Cramér's V stays fixed. In security telemetry, where millions of events are common, almost every pair of fields is significantly dependent, and the useful question is how strong the dependence is and whether it matters.

Association is not causation

The chi-square test answers a question about the joint distribution: do the categories of $X$ and $F$ occur together more or less often than chance would explain? Rejecting independence establishes a statistical association. It says nothing about the mechanism, and in particular it does not say that changing $X$ would change $F$. The test is symmetric in $X$ and $F$, while causation has a direction. An observed association can come from:

  • a causal effect of $X$ on $F$;
  • reverse causation, $F$ acting on $X$: ICMP flows are associated with Probe labels because scanning tools choose ICMP, not because the protocol causes attacks;
  • a confounder $Z$ that influences both: failed logins are frequent at night because brute-force bots run at night, not because of the hour itself;
  • selection bias: if only alerted flows are logged, the logged sample can show associations that do not exist in the traffic;
  • chance: with many tests some associations are false positives.

An example of confounding: antivirus and infections

The endpoint telemetry dataset pools 1,200 corporate and 800 home machines. Corporate machines have the antivirus more often (85% against 30%) and are much less exposed (4% against 22% infection probability). Within each environment the antivirus status and the infection are independent by construction: in this model the antivirus has no effect at all.

The pooled table shows a highly significant association ($p \approx 10^{-15}$): machines with the antivirus are infected in 8% of the cases, those without in 20%, so the antivirus looks very effective. Yet within each environment the association disappears, as the p-values show. The pooled association is produced entirely by the confounder $Z$, the environment, which drives both variables. Here $X$ and $F$ are dependent but conditionally independent given $Z$. A naive reading of the pooled table would credit the antivirus with an effect that, in this model, it does not have.

The opposite can also happen: a real causal effect can be hidden, or even reversed, in a pooled table (Simpson's paradox). Causal conclusions need more than a contingency table: a randomized experiment, where $X$ is assigned at random so that no confounder can be linked to it (for example, assigning security training at random before a phishing simulation), or an observational analysis that measures and adjusts for the confounders, for instance by stratifying as above, together with domain knowledge about the direction of the effect.

The code

The core of the computation, from the records to the p-value:

// 1. Contingency table: one pass over the raw records [x, f].
const counts = xs.map(() => fs.map(() => 0));
for (const [x, f] of records) counts[xIndex.get(x)][fIndex.get(f)] += 1;
const rowTotals = counts.map((row) => row.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0));       // n_i.
const colTotals = fs.map((_, j) => counts.reduce((a, row) => a + row[j], 0)); // n_.j
const n = rowTotals.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0);

// 2. Marginal and conditional frequencies.
const rowMarginal = rowTotals.map((t) => t / n);                               // f_i.
const rowProfiles = counts.map((row, i) => row.map((c) => c / rowTotals[i]));  // f(F | X)

// 3. Expected counts under independence and Pearson's statistic.
const E = rowTotals.map((r) => colTotals.map((c) => (r * c) / n));
let chi2 = 0;
counts.forEach((row, i) => row.forEach((o, j) => { chi2 += (o - E[i][j]) ** 2 / E[i][j]; }));
const df = (xs.length - 1) * (fs.length - 1);

// 4. p-value: upper tail of the chi-square distribution, Q(df/2, chi2/2).
const pValue = gammaQ(df / 2, chi2 / 2);
const cramersV = Math.sqrt(chi2 / (n * (Math.min(xs.length, fs.length) - 1)));

Conclusions

  • Two categorical variables are independent when the joint distribution is the product of the marginals, or equivalently when the conditional distribution of one does not depend on the value of the other. In a table this means equal row profiles and $n_{ij} = n_{i\cdot}n_{\cdot j}/n$.
  • Exact independence is never observed in a sample, so it is tested: Pearson's $\chi^2$ measures the distance between the observed and the expected table, and under $H_0$ it is approximately $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$. A permutation test gives the same answer without the approximation.
  • On the security datasets the test recovers the truth of the generating models: time of day and login outcome, and protocol and IDS label, are dependent; server and port parity are not; the phishing reaction depends on the department, more weakly.
  • The p-value measures evidence, Cramér's V measures strength, and the residuals locate the dependence. All three are needed for a sound interpretation.
  • A significant $\chi^2$ establishes association, not causation. The endpoint example shows a highly significant pooled association that vanishes once the confounder is taken into account.
