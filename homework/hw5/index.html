Riemann vs Lebesgue Integration and the Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

08/11/2026

Exercise 1

Riemann and Lebesgue Integration

Two ways of slicing an area

Riemann slices the domain. Split $[a,b]$ into cells $I_i$ of width $h$, choose a tag $t_i \in I_i$ in each and add up the rectangles:

$$R_n(f) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(t_i)\,h$$

$f$ is Riemann integrable when the sums converge to the same number for every choice of tags. Equivalently, the upper and lower sums, which use the supremum and the infimum of $f$ on each cell, must have the same limit.

Lebesgue slices the range. Split the values into levels $y_0 < y_1 < \dots < y_K$, take the preimage of each band and weigh the band by the measure $\mu$ (the total length) of that preimage:

$$E_j = \{x : y_j \le f(x) < y_{j+1}\}, \qquad L_K(f) = \sum_{j=0}^{K-1} \frac{y_j + y_{j+1}}{2}\,\mu(E_j)$$

A cell of the domain is an interval, but $E_j$ can be any measurable set: it can be scattered over many pieces, or even be a Cantor-like set. Replacing the tag by $y_j$ or by $y_{j+1}$ gives a lower and an upper sum on the same levels, and these differ by at most $\Delta y\,(b-a)$ when the levels have spacing $\Delta y$. This holds for every bounded measurable $f$, however irregular, which is the real gain of the definition.

With $G(y) = \mu\{x : f(x) \ge y\}$, the distribution of the values of $f$, the sum rearranges into the layer cake formula for $f \ge 0$:

$$\int_a^b f\,dx = \int_0^\infty G(y)\,dy$$

$\mu(E_j) = G(y_j) - G(y_{j+1})$, and summing by parts shows that $L_K$ with the midpoint tag is exactly the trapezoidal rule applied to $G$. The accuracy of $L_K$ therefore depends on how regular $G$ is, not on how regular $f$ is.

The two theories are consistent. If $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, it is Lebesgue integrable and the integrals coincide. By the Lebesgue criterion, a bounded $f$ is Riemann integrable exactly when its set of discontinuities has measure zero.

The numerical schemes

The Riemann scheme is the midpoint rule, because its tag is the natural one and its error is $O(h^2)$ on smooth functions. Lebesgue is less direct: one needs the measure of a preimage, which a computer does not know. Two schemes are used here (integration.js).

  • Exact preimages. For each level $y$, a fixed scan grid shows in which cells $f - y$ changes sign and bisection refines each crossing to machine precision. Then $G(y)$ is a sum of interval lengths. Jumps are handled too, since bisection converges to them.
  • Sampled preimages. Evaluate $f$ on $N$ midpoints and count how many values fall in each band, so that $\mu(E_j) \approx \text{count}_j \cdot h$. There is no root finding, but the accuracy cannot exceed that of the grid.
// Riemann: slice the domain, one evaluation per cell.
const riemannMidpoint = (f, a, b, n) => {
  const h = (b - a) / n;
  let sum = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i += 1) sum += f(a + (i + 0.5) * h);
  return sum * h;
};

// Lebesgue: slice the range, weigh each band by the measure of its preimage.
// G[j] = measure of {f >= levels[j]}, found with a scan grid and bisection.
let value = 0;
for (let j = 0; j < K; j += 1) {
  const measure = G[j] - G[j + 1];                       // measure of E_j
  value += 0.5 * (levels[j] + levels[j + 1]) * measure;  // midpoint tag
}
On a fixed grid the two ideas are the same sum

If the sampled scheme used, as the tag of each band, the mean of the sampled values in that band, then $\sum_j \text{count}_j\, h\, \overline{f}_j = h\sum_i f(x_i)$, the Riemann sum on the same grid. Grouping by value only reorders a finite sum. A difference between the schemes can come only from the tag or from knowing the preimages better than the grid does.

The sizes $n$ and $K$ are odd below. With even $n$ the point $x = 0.5$ would be a cell boundary and the midpoint rule would be exact on the step functions and on $|x-0.5|$ by luck, not by merit.

The two partitions on one function

Fewer levels make the structure clear. For $\sin x$ the preimage of every band except the top one is made of two separate pieces, one on each side: a Lebesgue rectangle has no reason to be contiguous. For $1/\sqrt x$ the picture is clipped at height 6, while the levels keep growing geometrically.

Accuracy, convergence and efficiency

Computing…

  • The limits agree. All the functions here are Riemann integrable (or improperly so, for $1/\sqrt x$) and every scheme approaches the exact value, as the theorem requires.
  • On smooth functions Riemann is better. Its order is 2 against about 3/2, and it pays one evaluation per cell while the exact-preimage scheme pays some 30-50 bisection evaluations for every crossing. To reach $10^{-3}$ on $\sin x$, the exact-preimage scheme costs a few hundred times more evaluations, the sampled one about thirty times more.
  • On piecewise linear functions and symmetric steps Lebesgue is exact, since $G$ is piecewise linear. The two symmetric steps are special: the errors of the two atoms cancel because they have equal weight. The step at $1/\pi$ has unequal weights, and both schemes fall back to order 1.
  • At a singularity Lebesgue wins clearly, with order about 1.5 against 0.5 and some 27 times fewer evaluations to reach $10^{-3}$. The next table isolates this case.

The unbounded function $1/\sqrt x$

Near $x=0$ a uniform grid has no resolution where $f$ is large: the first cell alone misses a share of the integral of order $\sqrt h$, so the error decays only like $n^{-1/2}$. The range sees the problem directly: $G(y) = \min(1, y^{-2})$ is known exactly and the levels can be packed where they are needed, with geometric spacing up to a height $K^2$. With uniform levels the same scheme is poor: reaching a high cut needs wide bands, and they are too coarse at low values, where $G$ changes fastest. The advantage comes from choosing the levels, not from the name of the method.

Computing…

Pathological functions

Grid sampling has no meaning here: a double is always a dyadic rational, so a naive isRational(x) would answer true on every point, $\sqrt 2$ and Math.random() included. What follows therefore works with tags described symbolically and with exact integer arithmetic.

Dirichlet's function: the sums do not converge

$f = \mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q}$ on $[0,1]$. Every cell contains rationals and irrationals, so the upper sum is 1 and the lower sum is 0 for every $n$. The tags $i/n$ are rational and the tags $(i + 1/\sqrt 2)/n$ are irrational, which gives Riemann sums that stay at 1 and at 0, and a mixed choice lands anywhere in between.

Computing…

The Lebesgue integral exists and is $0$. The rationals are countable, $\mathbb Q \cap [0,1] = \{q_1, q_2, \dots\}$; covering $q_k$ with an interval of length $\varepsilon\, 2^{-k}$ uses total length at most $\varepsilon$, for every $\varepsilon > 0$. Hence $\mu(\mathbb Q \cap [0,1]) = 0$ and

$$\int_{[0,1]} \mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q}\,d\mu = 1\cdot\mu(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]) + 0\cdot\mu([0,1]\setminus\mathbb Q) = 0$$

This is the sharpest difference between the two approaches. The range has only two values, so the Lebesgue computation needs just two measures; the domain has no useful partition at all.

Thomae's function: same value, opposite mechanism

$f(p/q) = 1/q$ in lowest terms, $f(0) = f(1) = 1$, $f = 0$ on the irrationals. It is continuous at every irrational and discontinuous at every rational, a null set, so it is Riemann integrable with integral 0. Here the sums can be computed exactly: the supremum on a cell is $1/q$, with $q$ the smallest denominator of a fraction in that cell, found with the Stern-Brocot tree.

Computing…

The upper sums vanish, but slowly: the column $U_n\sqrt n$ settles near a constant, i.e. $U_n \approx n^{-1/2}$, an order much lower than on regular functions. On the Lebesgue side the answer is immediate, because each set $\{f \ge t\}$ with $t > 0$ is finite:

All the positive values live on a null set and $\int f\,d\mu = 0$ with no limit to take.

The fat Cantor set: Riemann fails on a set of positive measure

Dirichlet's function has a Lebesgue integral equal to 0, so one might believe that non-integrable behaviour requires a negligible answer. The Smith-Volterra-Cantor set $C$ shows otherwise. Start from $[0,1]$ and at step $k$ remove the open middle gap of length $4^{-k}$ from each of the $2^{k-1}$ remaining intervals. The removed length is $\sum_k 2^{k-1}4^{-k} = 1/2$, so $\mu(C) = 1/2$, and $C$ contains no interval. For $f = \mathbf 1_C$ the gaps are dense, so the infimum on any cell is 0; the upper sum counts the cells touching $C$.

Computing…

The upper sum tends to $1/2$ and the lower sum is 0, so $f$ is not Riemann integrable: its discontinuities are exactly the points of $C$, which have positive measure. The Lebesgue integral is $\mu(C) = 1/2$. Tags chosen uniformly at random give about $1/2$, because a random point falls in $C$ with probability $\mu(C)$: Monte Carlo estimates the Lebesgue integral, and it does so without any regularity assumption.

Conclusions

  • For Riemann-integrable functions both approaches converge to the same value, as predicted, and the tables above confirm it.
  • As a numerical method Riemann is simpler and cheaper on smooth functions. Lebesgue needs a measure of each preimage, which is a harder problem than the integral itself unless $G$ is available.
  • The Lebesgue point of view pays off when the range is simpler than the domain: integrands with few values, level sets known in closed form, or singularities where the values rather than the positions tell where the integral lives.
  • As a definition Lebesgue is stronger. It assigns a value to Dirichlet's function and to $\mathbf 1_C$, where Riemann sums depend on the tags and have no limit, and its bracket $\Delta y\,(b-a)$ needs no regularity of $f$.

Exercise 2

Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

The function to compute

The standard normal density and its CDF are

$$\varphi(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-t^2/2}, \qquad \Phi(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} \varphi(t)\,dt$$

(see Hogg, McKean and Craig, Section 3.4, on the normal distribution). The constant is exact, because the integral over the whole line can be computed by squaring it and passing to polar coordinates:

$$\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2/2}dt\right)^{\!2} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\, r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi$$

This trick works only for the integral over all of $\mathbb R$. For a finite upper limit $z$ no such shortcut exists, and the next card explains why.

Why $\Phi$ is not an elementary function: Liouville's theorem

An elementary function is built from rational functions, exponentials, logarithms and algebraic functions by finitely many sums, products and compositions. The question is whether an elementary $F$ exists with $F' = e^{-t^2/2}$.

Theorem (Liouville, in the form due to Rosenlicht). Let $f$ and $g$ be rational functions with $g$ non-constant. Then $\int f\,e^{g}\,dt$ is elementary if and only if there is a rational function $R$ with

$$f = R' + g'\,R$$

The idea is that if $\int f e^g$ were elementary, it would have the form $R\,e^g$: differentiating gives $(R' + g'R)e^g$, and comparing with $f e^g$ yields the condition.

Here $f = 1$ and $g = -t^2/2$, so the question becomes whether a rational $R$ exists with

$$R'(t) - t\,R(t) = 1$$
  • $R$ has no poles. A pole of order $m \ge 1$ at $t_0$ makes $R'$ have a pole of order $m+1$, while $tR$ has one of order at most $m$. The left side would be singular at $t_0$, but the right side is the constant 1.
  • $R$ is then a polynomial, and $R \ne 0$ since $0 \ne 1$. If its degree is $d$, then $tR$ has degree $d+1 \ge 1$ and $R'$ has degree $d-1$, so the left side has degree $d+1 \ge 1$ and cannot equal a constant.

No such $R$ exists, so $e^{-t^2/2}$ has no elementary antiderivative and $\Phi$ has no closed form in terms of elementary functions. This is a statement about the form of $\Phi$ and not about its existence: $\Phi$ is smooth and well defined, and a table of its values is perfectly legitimate.

The error function, and why numerics are still needed

The mathematical response is to give this integral a name. The error function and its complement are

$$\operatorname{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^x e^{-u^2}du, \qquad \operatorname{erfc}(x) = 1 - \operatorname{erf}(x)$$

With $t = \sqrt2\,u$ the CDF has the exact representation

$$\boxed{\Phi(z) = \frac12\left[1 + \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt 2}\right)\right] = \frac12\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt 2}\right)}$$

This is an exact identity, but it only moves the problem: $\operatorname{erf}$ is defined by the same kind of integral. A special function is an analytical tool that gives properties, identities and series; to get digits one still needs an algorithm. Elementary functions such as $e^x$ are also computed by approximation, so the difference is only that they are provided by every math library, while $\operatorname{erf}$ often is not. JavaScript, for one, has no Math.erf.

Numerical approaches

Numerical integration

Since $\Phi(z) = \tfrac12 + \int_0^z \varphi$, the trapezoidal rule (error $O(n^{-2})$) and Simpson's rule (error $O(n^{-4})$) apply directly.

Power series

Integrating the exponential series term by term gives

$$\Phi(z) = \frac12 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k\, z^{2k+1}}{2^k\, k!\,(2k+1)}$$

It converges for every $z$, but the terms alternate and grow up to about $e^{z^2/2}$ in size before they decrease. In floating point that is catastrophic cancellation, the same issue as the naive variance formula of Homework 4.

The cure is an all-positive series. Let $S(z) = z + \frac{z^3}{3} + \frac{z^5}{3\cdot 5} + \dots$. Then $S' = 1 + zS$, so $(e^{-z^2/2}S)' = e^{-z^2/2}$, and

$$\Phi(z) = \frac12 + \varphi(z)\left(z + \frac{z^3}{3} + \frac{z^5}{3\cdot 5} + \frac{z^7}{3\cdot5\cdot7} + \dots\right)$$

No term cancels another, and the sum is accurate to rounding for the central part of the distribution.

Tails and a continued fraction

In the lower tail $\Phi(-z)$ is tiny, and computing it as $\tfrac12 - (\dots)$ loses every digit, whatever the accuracy of the bracket. For $x > 0$ Laplace's continued fraction gives the tail directly:

$$\operatorname{erfc}(x) = \frac{e^{-x^2}}{\sqrt\pi}\;\cfrac{1}{x + \cfrac{1/2}{x + \cfrac{2/2}{x + \cfrac{3/2}{x + \dots}}}}$$

It needs fewer iterations as $x$ grows, which is the opposite behaviour of the series, so the two complement each other.

The proposed method

$\Phi(z) = \tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(-z/\sqrt2)$, with $\operatorname{erf}$ from the positive series for $x < 1.75$ and $\operatorname{erfc}$ from the continued fraction, evaluated with the modified Lentz algorithm, for $x \ge 1.75$ (normal-cdf.js). The switch is at the point where the two costs and accuracies balance. $e^{-x^2}$ is split as $e^{-w^2}e^{-(x-w)(x+w)}$, with $w$ a multiple of $1/16$, to avoid the rounding error of $x^2$ in the far tail.

const erfSeries = (x) => {          // erf(x), 0 <= x < 1.75
  let term = x, sum = x, k = 0;
  while (Math.abs(term) > 1e-17 * sum) {
    term *= (2 * x * x) / (2 * k + 3);
    sum += term;
    k += 1;
  }
  return (2 / Math.sqrt(Math.PI)) * expMinusSquare(x) * sum;
};

const erfcFraction = (x) => {       // erfc(x), x >= 1.75, modified Lentz
  let f = x, c = x, d = 0;
  for (let k = 1; k < 2000; k += 1) {
    d = 1 / (x + (k / 2) * d);
    c = x + (k / 2) / c;
    const delta = c * d;
    f *= delta;
    if (Math.abs(delta - 1) < 1e-16) break;
  }
  return expMinusSquare(x) / Math.sqrt(Math.PI) / f;
};

const erfc = (x) => x < 0 ? 2 - erfc(-x) : x < 1.75 ? 1 - erfSeries(x) : erfcFraction(x);
const normalCdf = (z) => 0.5 * erfc(-z / Math.SQRT2);

Comparison with a reference from the web

The table of the 68-95-99.7 rule on Wikipedia lists $P(|Z| \le z) = \operatorname{erf}(z/\sqrt 2)$ to 15 decimals. Since $\Phi(z) = (1 + P)/2$, the two can be compared directly.

The agreement is within one unit of the last printed digit, which is the rounding of the source combined with that of $2\Phi - 1$ in double precision. A printed table with 15 decimals cannot test the tail, though: $\Phi(-10) \approx 7.6\times 10^{-24}$ would simply read 0. For the tail I computed a separate reference with 900-digit decimal arithmetic. Python's decimal sums the positive series, for which more digits remove every rounding concern, and it matches math.erfc to the accuracy of that library.

The erf-based method keeps at least 12 correct significant digits down to $z = -37.5$, close to the smallest positive double. The mild degradation there is not an algorithmic defect: $\Phi$ has condition number about $z^2$ in the tail, so the rounding of $z/\sqrt 2$ alone costs that much.

Convergence and stability

The series converges faster than any power of the number of terms, but for large $|z|$ it stalls at the level of the rounding of its largest term:

At $z=10$ the alternating series has terms of size $10^{19}$ and returns a meaningless result, while the positive series with a similar number of terms is correct to rounding.

Three distinct behaviours appear. Simpson and the positive series compute $\tfrac12 - (\dots)$, so their relative error explodes in the tail once $\Phi(-z)$ is below their absolute accuracy. Hastings' polynomial approximates the tail directly, so its relative error stays bounded and grows slowly, up to about 2% at $z = 10$. Only the erf-based method stays near rounding level along the whole range.

Efficiency

Computing…

  • Quadrature is simple and general, but it pays hundreds of evaluations per call and still fails in the tail.
  • Hastings' polynomial is the fastest, with constant cost and absolute error below $7.5\times 10^{-8}$. It is enough for many statistical uses, such as a p-value printed with three digits.
  • The erf-based method costs a fraction of a microsecond and gives nearly full double precision in the centre and in the tails, with relative errors around $10^{-14}$. This is the right choice when the CDF is called many times or when small tail probabilities matter, for example when assessing how extreme an anomaly score is in a detection system, where $\Phi(-8)$ and $\Phi(-10)$ differ by about eight orders of magnitude.

Conclusions

  • $\Phi$ has no elementary closed form, by Liouville's theorem. The equation $R' - tR = 1$ has no rational solution.
  • $\operatorname{erf}$ gives an exact representation, $\Phi(z) = \tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(-z/\sqrt2)$, but a representation is not an algorithm. Numerics are still required, as they are for any special function.
  • The choice of algorithm matters as much as the formula. The same quantity, computed with an alternating series, a quadrature or a continued fraction, ranges from no correct digit to full double precision, depending on $z$.
  • The combination of a positive series and a continued fraction agrees with the web reference within one unit of its last printed digit and with a 900-digit reference to at least 12 significant digits everywhere, in a few hundred nanoseconds.
